Slitter Rewinder Factory Acceptance Test Checklist for Overseas Buyers

A slitter rewinder FAT should prove more than basic machine movement. Before shipment, overseas buyers should verify slit edge quality, rewind stability, safety functions, control response, test records and installation readiness using the real material and realistic production conditions.

Published June 7, 2026 | By HDPTH Technical Editorial Team

Short answer: The best slitter rewinder factory acceptance test checklist focuses on four things: whether the machine can run your actual material at an agreed condition, whether finished rolls and slit edges meet your quality target, whether controls and safety functions respond correctly, and whether shipment documents and site-preparation data are complete before dispatch.

Why FAT matters for slitter rewinder projects

For overseas buyers, the cost of finding a problem after shipment is much higher than finding it before dispatch. Once the machine has been packed, exported, customs-cleared, installed and wired into plant utilities, even a modest correction can consume weeks. That is why a factory acceptance test matters so much on slitting and rewinding projects. It compresses technical review, quality confirmation and shipment readiness into one controlled checkpoint.

This is especially important when the line will process demanding roll materials such as nonwoven, paper, PE film or PET film. Slit quality, roll hardness, telescoping, web wandering and operator repeatability are not theoretical topics. They directly affect waste, downtime and downstream packaging or converting efficiency. A FAT gives purchasing, engineering and plant teams a shared basis for release.

HDPTH's product pages already show why the checklist cannot be generic. The high-speed slitting machine range covers project-based width, knife and speed configurations, while the rewinding machine range covers different widths, diameters and utility requirements. A FAT should therefore be tied to the agreed configuration rather than a broad brochure promise.

What buyers should prepare before the FAT date

The strongest FAT starts before anyone enters the workshop. If the supplier and buyer only discuss testing on the same day, the result is often vague. The better approach is to agree in writing on the test material, the required widths, the intended finished roll structure, the target speed and the evidence that will be recorded.

  • Material identity: substrate type, GSM or thickness, elasticity, surface condition and any special edge behavior.
  • Parent roll data: width, diameter, core size and expected unwinding condition.
  • Finished-roll target: slit widths, rewind diameter, core size, roll hardness expectation and downstream use.
  • Test conditions: target speed, acceptable stable speed, acceleration and stop behavior to be demonstrated.
  • Inspection method: who checks edge quality, side-face quality, web alignment, alarms and operator interface.
  • Evidence package: video angles, photos, final checklist, punch list and shipment-release document set.

If the machine is being purchased for multiple product types, choose the most technically demanding realistic case rather than the easiest material available in the factory. That does not mean trying to test every future SKU in one day. It means using the material that reveals whether the control method, knife arrangement and winding logic really fit the project.

1. Running test under agreed conditions

A slitter rewinder FAT should include more than a short low-speed demonstration. Ask the supplier to show startup, acceleration to the agreed condition, stable running, deceleration and stop behavior. If the project involves multiple slit widths or recipe changes, ask how those changes are handled and how much operator intervention is required.

Record exactly what material was used, what parent-roll condition was available and what speed was actually shown. If the machine is described commercially as high speed, the FAT record should distinguish maximum design speed from demonstrated stable speed on the buyer's material. That distinction matters because many future disputes come from confusing those two numbers.

2. Cut quality and finished-roll quality

Buyers often focus first on speed, but speed is not the final output. The commercial result is the finished roll. During the FAT, inspect slit edges for fuzz, dust, edge lifting, width inconsistency or visible damage to the substrate. Then inspect the rewound rolls for side-face alignment, winding consistency, telescoping risk and visual uniformity across positions.

When the line is intended for hygiene, wipes or medical-related nonwoven processing, small edge or winding defects can later create packaging trouble, roll changeover problems or downstream waste. That is why roll inspection should be part of the release decision, not an afterthought after the machine stops.

3. Controls, safety and operator repeatability

For a buyer, a machine is not truly accepted if it only works when the supplier's most experienced technician stands next to it. Review the operator interface carefully. Can the line recipe be stored? Are alarms understandable? Can the operator identify whether a problem comes from tension, knife position, web alignment or rewinding pressure? These questions affect daily uptime after installation.

Safety checks should also be documented. International machinery practice usually requires the buyer to review guarding, electrical basics and energy-isolation points before shipment. Authoritative references such as ISO 12100, IEC 60204-1 and OSHA's machine-guarding and lockout/tagout requirements are useful here because they frame the review around risk reduction, electrical equipment and servicing safety rather than only around mechanical output.

4. Documents, punch list and shipment release

Many export problems are document problems. Before approving shipment, ask for the final configuration summary, utility requirements, electrical information, spare-parts list, operation guidance and the packing plan. If the FAT produces open items, log them clearly with ownership and target close date. A weak FAT ends with verbal promises. A strong FAT ends with a written release decision and a written list of remaining actions, if any.

That kind of visibility is useful, but it should still be connected to project-specific FAT records before shipment approval.

FAT versus SAT: what changes after the machine arrives

Factory acceptance testing and site acceptance testing serve different purposes. FAT shows that the supplier built the machine correctly and can demonstrate performance before shipping. SAT shows whether the machine still performs correctly after transport, installation and connection to the buyer's real utilities, operators and production environment. Both checkpoints matter.

For SAT to go smoothly, the buyer should use the FAT period to confirm installation preparation. That includes floor space, anchoring, electrical standard, compressed-air readiness, lifting method, parent-roll handling, operator availability and sample material. Local standards may also affect guarding, signage and energy-isolation procedures after installation. If these topics are ignored at FAT stage, the SAT often becomes a delayed engineering discussion instead of a simple confirmation step.

On HDPTH's current product pages, several utility and parameter references can help the buyer prepare early. For example, the rewinding-machine page shows 0.6 MPa air-supply requirements for certain configurations, while the slitting-machine page lists project-based ranges for width, speed and power. Those details should be reconciled with the actual ordered machine before shipment, not after it reaches the plant.

Red flags that buyers should not ignore

One red flag is a supplier who avoids testing with the real material or gives only a very short, carefully limited demo. Another is a supplier who will show machine motion but not finished-roll inspection. A third is missing clarity on what is standard, what is optional and what has been modified from the original quotation.

Buyers should also be careful when a FAT checklist contains only broad statements such as "machine runs well." That wording is too weak for an export equipment release. A useful FAT record states the material tested, the slit widths used, the run condition demonstrated, the quality observations made and the documents handed over. If those details are absent, the buyer is accepting uncertainty rather than accepting a machine.

Questions to ask before signing off the FAT

  • What exact material and roll condition were used for the demonstration?
  • What stable running condition was demonstrated and how was it recorded?
  • Which slit widths and finished-roll targets were checked during the test?
  • Which guarding, emergency-stop and interlock functions were reviewed?
  • What punch-list items remain open, and who closes them before shipment?
  • What site utilities, spares and tools must be ready before installation?

If a supplier answers these questions clearly, the buyer usually also gets clearer quotations, smoother shipping decisions and less commissioning friction later. If the answers stay vague, the buyer should slow down and resolve the uncertainty before authorizing dispatch.

Buyer FAQs

What is the purpose of a slitter rewinder FAT?

A factory acceptance test confirms before shipment that the slitter rewinder matches the agreed configuration and can demonstrate stable running, cut quality, rewind quality, controls and basic safety functions.

Should buyers use their own sample material during FAT?

Yes. Buyer-supplied material gives a more reliable result because slit edge quality, tension response and roll formation depend heavily on the actual substrate, width range and finished-roll target.

What running conditions should be recorded during the FAT?

Record startup, acceleration, steady running, width change if applicable, deceleration, stop behavior, finished-roll condition and the material details used for the test.

What documents should be checked before shipment?

Check the final configuration summary, packing list, electrical information, spare-parts list, operating guidance, test records and installation utility requirements before approving shipment.

How is FAT different from SAT?

FAT is performed at the supplier's factory before shipment, while SAT is performed after delivery and installation to confirm the machine still performs correctly in the buyer's actual plant conditions.
