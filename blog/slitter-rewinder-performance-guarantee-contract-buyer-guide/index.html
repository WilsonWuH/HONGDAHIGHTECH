Performance Guarantees in Slitter Rewinder Contracts: Speed, Tolerance, Waste, Availability, Remedies and Warranty Terms

A guaranteed number protects you only when the contract also defines how it is measured, under what conditions, and what happens when it is missed.

Published September 22, 2026 | By HDPTH Technical Editorial Team

Short answer: a guarantee is only worth the test behind it. Define every figure with method, conditions, duration and sample size; name the material; align factory and site acceptance; build a remedy ladder of correction, price reduction and rejection; and keep the warranty clause narrow on exclusions and generous on spare part availability.

Most buyers spend six months negotiating price and twenty minutes negotiating performance. The resulting clause promises "production speed up to 400 m/min" without saying with which material, over what run length, or what happens at 380. When the machine lands short, everyone discovers that an unmeasurable promise is unenforceable, however sincerely it was made. This guide sets out how to write guarantees that survive contact with commissioning — measurable, testable, and linked to clear remedies.

Turning promises into measurable definitions

Start from the figures that decide profitability: line speed on the hardest material you actually run, slit width tolerance at stated conditions, wound-in defect or waste rate per parent roll, roll length and diameter accuracy, changeover time for a defined format change, and availability over an agreed period. Each needs a test method, duration, sampling plan and pass criterion. "Speed" means sustained running for a stated period without operator intervention, not a peak reading on a trend screen; "tolerance" needs measurement instrument, sample count and statistical treatment, exactly as discussed in our guide to slit width tolerance.

Availability and changeover: the two easiest to fake

Availability needs the strictest wording, because almost every failure of definition favours the seller. State what counts as uptime, which causes are excluded, how long the observation period runs, and whether planned maintenance, tooling changes and material waits sit inside it. Insist that machine-caused stops after start-up are counted, however short. Changeover has the mirror problem: it is easy to measure from a prepared state. Define the starting state precisely — machine stopped after the previous order, knives in place — together with crew size, tooling and the target condition.

Test conditions and the material question

A performance figure is meaningless without its input material. Name the grade, grammage, width, parent roll diameter, core specification and known variability, then state who supplies the material, how it is conditioned and stored, and what happens if it deviates. Add ambient conditions, utilities, staffing and operator experience. Decide explicitly whether start-up scrap, threading losses and roll changes count within the measurement window. Ambiguity here causes more stalled acceptances than any technical failure, because every deviation later becomes an argument about whether conditions were met.

Remedies: build a ladder, not a cliff

A guarantee without a remedy is a press release. Workable contracts use a three-step ladder: first a defined correction period in which the supplier repairs or adjusts at its own cost, with a limited number of attempts; then a price reduction proportional to the shortfall, usually capped as a percentage of contract value; then, if a fundamental guarantee still fails, a right to reject and refund. Penalty percentages need clear caps and exclusions for causes outside supplier control, and they must never replace the duty to repair. Also define who pays for repeated site visits and retesting.

FAT and site acceptance working together

Factory acceptance isolates machine capability: representative material, supplier technicians present, no production pressure. Site acceptance confirms the same capability in your reality: your material, your operators, your utilities. The contract should therefore use identical figures and methods at both stages, state which result governs final acceptance, specify what happens if the two disagree, and spell out the consequence of waiving FAT. This is why the FAT checklist should be written by whoever will later defend acceptance, not attached as an afterthought.

Warranty period, response and exclusions

Set the start trigger precisely — commissioning or provisional acceptance rather than shipment — together with duration, repair or replace obligations, response time and whether travel and labour are included. Keep exclusions narrow: consumables, misuse, unapproved modifications, normal wear. What usually matters more than an extra six months of cover is spare parts availability for a defined number of years, because obsolete components rather than failed ones are what eventually stop a line. Require a priced spares list and a stated support horizon.

Clause checklist before signing

  • Every guaranteed figure has method, conditions, duration, sample size and pass criterion.
  • Material specifications, supplier and conditioning are stated.
  • Remedy ladder defined: correction period, price reduction formula with cap, rejection right.
  • FAT and site acceptance use the same figures and methods; governing result identified.
  • Warranty trigger date, response time, included costs and narrow exclusions stated.
  • Spare parts availability horizon and priced list attached as an annex.

Frequently asked questions

Which performance figures deserve a contractual guarantee?

The ones that decide whether your orders are profitable: guaranteed line speed on the named difficult material, slit width tolerance at defined conditions, wound-in waste or defect rate per parent roll, roll length or diameter accuracy, and availability measured over an agreed period. Everything guaranteed must be defined as a test with a stated method, duration, sample size and material. A number without a method is marketing; number, method, conditions and remedy together form a clause you can enforce.

How should test conditions and material be written into the contract?

Name the material to be used for each test: grade, grammage, width, roll diameter, core specification and any known variability, plus who supplies it and how it is stored. Agree ambient conditions, utilities, staffing and operator experience. State the test duration and whether start-up scrap and threading losses count. If the buyer supplies operators, say whose operators run the trial. Ambiguity here is the most common reason guarantees stall, because every later deviation becomes an argument about whether the conditions were met.

What remedy structure works when a machine misses its guarantee?

A ladder rather than a cliff. First, a defined period and number of attempts for the supplier to correct the machine at their cost. Then a price reduction proportional to the shortfall, often capped as a percentage of the contract value. Then a right of rejection and refund if the machine fails a fundamental guarantee after the correction period. Penalty percentages need a clear cap and exclusions for causes outside the supplier’s control, and they should never replace the obligation to repair.

Should FAT and site acceptance test the same guarantees?

They should test the same numbers with the same methods, and the contract should say what changes between them. Factory tests usually run on representative material with supplier technicians present, which isolates machine capability. Site tests then confirm the same capability on actual production material, operator staffing and utilities. State which results govern final acceptance, what happens if the two disagree, and how a FAT waiver transfers risk. Otherwise the strongest evidence, generated at the factory, is discarded at the plant.

What belongs in the warranty period clause?

Duration and start trigger, usually commissioning or provisional acceptance rather than shipment. Repair versus replace obligations, response time for a technician, how parts are shipped and at whose cost, and whether travel and labour are included. Then exclusions that should be narrow: consumables, misuse, unapproved modifications and normal wear. Spare part availability over a defined number of years is often more valuable than an extra six months of warranty, since availability of obsolete parts is what actually keeps a line running.
