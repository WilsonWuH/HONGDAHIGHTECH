Payment Terms for Slitter Rewinder Purchases: A Buyer’s Guide to Milestones, Letters of Credit and Guarantees

A machine is bought twice — with a purchase order and with the payment schedule. The second decides how much of the first you still control.

Published October 8, 2026 | By HDPTH Technical Editorial Team

Short answer: A slitter rewinder order is paid in milestones tied to project stages: an order deposit of 20–30 percent, progress payments on major components, a substantial instalment after the factory acceptance test, and a small retention until the machine runs at your site. Letters of credit pay against documents, telegraphic transfer against invoices — bank guarantees cover what the structure leaves exposed. Make the supplier’s cash flow follow the acceptance milestones, and most disputes never start.

Negotiations for a slitter rewinder often end where they should not: the commercial annex. Speeds, widths and knife systems get weeks of attention; the payment schedule gets a sentence. That is backwards: on a six-to-twelve-month machinery project, the payment structure is the buyer’s strongest lever. Money released at the right milestones funds supplier costs while keeping leverage where you carry risk — acceptance, commissioning, performance. This guide covers typical structures, cross-border instruments, and the clauses that tie money to the machine.

Why machinery payments differ from commodity buying

A slitter rewinder is not bought from stock. It is engineered to your widths and materials, built over months, and proved at the factory and at your site. The supplier invests engineering and long-lead parts before anything ships; the buyer invests cash before anything produces. Payment terms allocate this mutual exposure. Terms too supplier-heavy leave the buyer financing acceptance risk; terms too buyer-heavy push financing costs into the price. The workable middle ground is milestone payments matched to actual cost events on both sides.

Typical milestone structures

MilestoneTypical shareWhat it fundsBuyer protection
Order signature20–30%Engineering, design review, long-lead partsAdvance payment guarantee on the deposit
Material release10–20%Main structure, drive componentsProgress evidence, third-party inspection option
Pre-shipment after FAT40–60%Completion, packing, freight preparationSigned FAT report as payment condition
SAT / final acceptance5–10% retentionCommissioning support, warranty startPerformance bond valid through acceptance

The pattern to remember: deposits fund real early costs and are acceptable with a guarantee; the large instalment belongs after the factory acceptance test; and a retention keeps both parties attentive through commissioning. If a quotation compresses everything into “70% against shipping documents, 30% after commissioning”, negotiate the split: the shipping trigger pays whether or not the machine passed FAT, quietly transferring acceptance risk back to you.

Letters of credit versus telegraphic transfer

Cross-border machinery deals run on two main instruments. Telegraphic transfer is a direct bank payment against an invoice — fast, cheap, trust-dependent. A letter of credit is a conditional bank undertaking: the supplier is paid when conforming documents are presented, under UCP 600. The subtlety: an LC pays against documents, not performance. A single shipment LC gives almost no quality protection — the bank will pay against clean shipping papers for a machine that failed its tests. For equipment, use milestone LCs: each instalment triggered by documents you define — signed FAT report, third-party inspection certificate, shipping documents. That converts the LC from a formality into a control point. T/T with bank guarantees offers similar protection with less document handling.

Bank guarantees: the instruments that matter

Two guarantees cover the residual risk. An advance payment guarantee secures your deposit: if the project collapses, it allows recovery of the advance rather than an unsecured claim. A performance bond — 5 to 10 percent of contract value, valid until final acceptance — backs the contractual performance figures. Check three details: the issuing bank (a first-class bank you recognise), the expiry date (covering acceptance plus a buffer for delays), and the trigger (on-demand guarantees pay on first call, which gives them value). These instruments dovetail with the safeguards in our performance guarantee guide and the delivery logistics in the Incoterms guide.

Linking payments to FAT and SAT

The most valuable sentence in the commercial annex: “payment of X percent is due upon successful completion of FAT as defined in Annex N.” This clause converts the acceptance regime — the FAT checklist and the site acceptance test guide — into the supplier’s own cash-flow incentive. Define what “successful” means: performance figures, test run duration, and a sign-off procedure with a deadline for correcting open items. Release the retention against site acceptance or a defined trouble-free period, and commissioning requests get answered promptly. And write the FAT criteria first: if they are vague, the payment schedule inherits the vagueness.

Currency, cost and cash-flow planning

Finally, the mechanical details. Agree the contract currency and who carries exchange risk between signature and the last milestone — a twelve-month project can swing several percent — real money — and hedging the milestones is a routine treasury exercise. Confirm who pays bank charges — often split. Check whether guarantee and LC fees are priced in or added later. Plan internal cash flow against the milestones, including the deposit long before shipment. For buyers weighing the purchase against alternatives, our lease versus purchase guide interacts directly with this payment structure. Where the machine is near-standard, some suppliers offer stock or nearly complete units on shorter terms — a different risk profile worth asking about during the factory tour.

Frequently asked questions

What payment structure is typical for a slitter rewinder order?

Custom-built machinery is usually paid in three to four milestones: an order deposit of 20–30 percent releasing engineering and long-lead parts, a second instalment when main components are procured, and 30–50 percent at pre-shipment after successful FAT. A retention of 5–10 percent until commissioning or SAT is common. Milestones that match real costs on both sides survive negotiation.

How does a letter of credit work for machinery purchases?

An irrevocable letter of credit is your bank’s conditional promise to pay the supplier against documents — not against the machine. Milestone-based LCs triggered by documents like inspection certificates and FAT reports are more protective than a shipment LC, which pays against a bill of lading whether or not the machine passed testing. Under UCP 600, banks deal in documents only, so the documents you require define the protection you get.

Should I ever pay 100 percent before shipment?

No — and expect a legitimate supplier to agree. Full pre-shipment payment removes every incentive around acceptance, commissioning and spares support, and leaves you with an unsecured claim if the machine does not perform. If a supplier insists, treat it as a red flag and demand an advance payment guarantee from their bank until the machine is accepted.

What bank guarantees should accompany the payments?

Two instruments cover the main risks. An advance payment guarantee protects your deposit: if the supplier fails to deliver, your bank calls it and recovers the advance. A performance bond — typically 5–10 percent of contract value, valid until final acceptance — gives recourse if the machine misses the contractual guarantees. Insist on a bank acceptable to you, on demand, with expiry covering acceptance plus a buffer.

How should payment milestones link to FAT and SAT?

Directly and in writing. The pre-shipment instalment falls due only after the factory acceptance test concludes with a signed FAT report; the retention after site acceptance or a defined commissioning period. The supplier’s cash flow then tracks acceptance milestones instead of the calendar — never let payment and acceptance schedules drift apart.
